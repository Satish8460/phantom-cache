# Chapter specifications

From the owner's brief. Each writer reads the style file plus their own chapter here, and skims the others to avoid overlap.

Judge-question coverage the owner requires across the handbook:
- Fundamentals: why caching; why distributed; why not the database directly; what caching introduces.
- Architecture: explain it; walk a request; cache miss; origin unavailable; how nodes communicate.
- Technology: why Rust, Tokio, Axum; why not Go or C#; why partitioned mutex buckets; concurrency implications.
- Algorithms: Weighted Rendezvous; invalidation; epochs; coalescing; hot-key overload; time and space complexity.
- Distributed: node failure; lost invalidation; can two nodes serve different values; consistency guarantees; partitions.
- Security: unauthorised access; tenant isolation; malicious request; resource exhaustion; node-to-node security.
- Innovation: why novel; why not Redis or Memcached; what is the actual intelligence; strongest contribution; evidence; weaknesses versus established products.
- Evaluation: measuring performance; benchmark fairness; reproducing an experiment; simulation versus real; what to improve.

Every chapter must contain judge boxes, not only the last.

## Chapter 1: The Problem and the Vision
Teach: what caching is; why applications need it; hits and misses; latency, throughput, origin load; limits of traditional caching; cache stampedes, hot keys, stale data, invalidation problems; why distributed caching becomes necessary; what PhantomCache aims to solve; its three pillars (1 Distributed Systems Engineering, 2 Workload Intelligence, 3 Product Excellence) with an honest status of each; intended users and practical use cases. Explain the problem BEFORE the solution. Sources: README.md, docs/handoff/, docs/research/vision/phantomcache_three_pillar_product_direction.md and product_vision_vs_implementation_audit.md, docs/STATUS.md, CLAUDE.md. Each pillar must be tied to real code and evidence and to what is NOT yet proven.

## Chapter 2: System Architecture
Explain: major components and responsibilities; how they communicate; HTTP request flow; cache lookup and response flow; origin interaction; data flow; distributed communication; node and key relationships; configuration and deployment (config keys in src/config.rs, packaging/, any Docker or deployment files: verify what exists and do not describe deployment that does not exist); Phantom Lab's relationship to the real system. Provide architecture diagrams and request-sequence diagrams (hit; miss with coalescing; stale handling if implemented; origin failure; DELETE with cluster fan-out) from the CURRENT code (src/main.rs, src/api/server.rs, src/core/*, src/dist/*, src/lab*, ui/). Give both viewpoints: the external user and the internal engineer. Verify the binaries (src/bin/*, main) and what runs where; the ports; the HTTP routes (list them from the router definitions); auth boundaries at a high level (detail in Chapter 6).

## Chapter 3: Technology Stack and Engineering Decisions
Cover Rust, Tokio, Axum (and hyper and tower), async programming, concurrency primitives (std::sync Mutex and RwLock, atomics, Arc, channels as actually used), data structures (HashMap, Vec, VecDeque, BTreeMap, bitmaps, histograms as actually used), the TLS and mTLS stack (check Cargo.toml and src/security), the JWT library, serde, Docker and deployment (what exists), the React and Vite frontend (ui/), and testing and benchmarking tools (cargo test, tests/, benches/, proptest if present, node:test, CDP scripts). For each: 1 what it is in simple English; 2 why PhantomCache needs it; 3 why it was selected (the recorded decision if one exists in docs/research/adr or elsewhere, otherwise say it is not recorded); 4 alternatives; 5 why alternatives were not selected, only where evidence supports it; 6 how it is used (file::symbol); 7 advantages and limits. Read Cargo.toml and Cargo.lock for real dependency facts (versions, features) and note the supply-chain observations from docs/release/g4/cycle1_lab_intelligence_deps_review.md (verify them). Include the concurrency model: where std Mutex is used and the rule not to hold it across await. Judge boxes: why Rust, Tokio, Axum, not Go or C#, why sharded mutexes, concurrency implications, why React and plain CSS.

## Chapter 4: Core Cache Engine and Algorithms
Depth chapter. Cover from src/core/*: storage and lookup; hash maps and key-value structures; partitioned (sharded) mutex buckets and how a key maps to a shard; lock contention; concurrency versus parallelism; TTL and expiration (the freshness engine, RFC 9111-style revalidation, the stale cap PHANTOM_MAX_STALE_MS, the default-TTL wiring status); eviction (SIEVE default, LRU, S3-FIFO: explain each algorithm, then what is actually implemented, complexity, and the G4A finding on S3-FIFO; check against the CLAUDE.md claim of O(1) amortised); request coalescing and single-flight (src/core/coalesce.rs); cache stampede prevention; origin protection (the concurrency gate; AIMD enforcing only in canary or active: verify); invalidation; epoch version checks (tenant and global epochs, the epoch_snapshot RwLock, the overflow fallback); fencing and race prevention (generation, op_gen, finalize_fetch; the G4A-01 stale-body race: current status from docs/research/g4a01_race/ and the disposition register; the sandbox proposal is NOT integrated); memory management and byte accounting (I1 to I8, audit_bytes); lock-order rules. For each mechanism: the computer-science concept, why it is needed, how it works (pseudocode and a worked example with numbers), where it is in the code, complexity and performance, and failure modes. Judge boxes on sharded mutexes, coalescing, epochs, fencing, eviction, complexity, hot keys.

## Chapter 5: Distributed Systems and Workload Intelligence
Cover: distributed caching fundamentals; node membership and communication (src/dist/node.rs, session.rs, pool.rs, frame.rs, router.rs, anti_entropy.rs; the mTLS cluster; static membership: verify); weighted rendezvous hashing (explain the math with a worked example, and check what is actually implemented: are weights constant? DELETE-only routing; GET is local-only: state it precisely); key distribution; rebalancing implications; invalidation propagation (fan-out, ack epochs, the RepairLedger, anti-entropy; owed invalidations held only in memory are lost on an issuer crash: M3 durability status from docs/research/m3_durability/, sandbox not integrated); epochs and versioning across nodes; consistency guarantees and limits (what is guaranteed and what is not; the failure model; do not claim linearizability or bounded staleness without the bound and a test); node failure and recovery (heartbeats do not change state; NODE_FAILURE is a logical flag: verify); partitions and communication failures; cross-node concurrency; the peer GetRequest stub and DR-029 and DR-027. THEN workload intelligence: hot-key detection (what exists in src/intel/: whether sketch.rs CountMinSketch is used, workload.rs), workload patterns, telemetry (AtomicLogLinearHistogram, bucket count and error bound: verify), statistical and probabilistic mechanisms, adaptive policies, src/intelligence/* and src/control/pie.rs: explain the ACTUAL algorithm (AIMD, shadow evaluation, canary, watchdog), what is on the serving path versus library-only, the legacy PIE stub, and the negative M6 result (H1 NOT_SUPPORTED, sealed). Do not call adaptive mechanisms "AI". Be precise about local versus cross-node behaviour. Mark everything IMPLEMENTED, TESTED, PARTIAL, PROTOTYPE ONLY, DESIGNED BUT NOT IMPLEMENTED or DEFERRED.

## Chapter 6: Security, Reliability and Performance (mandatory, substantial)
Investigate and explain: authentication and authorisation (JWT: roles and claims, revocation absent, nbf and jti gaps, SEC-M7-09/10/11; check the current code in src/security and src/api/server.rs); management and API access (/api/control, /api/events, DELETE); peer-to-peer trust (mTLS, SAN and fingerprint admission, static membership); input validation; request body limits and timeouts (G4B-01 pre-authentication body read); connection management; tenant isolation (tenant claim, key namespacing, __default__; G4A-19 versus the G4B refutation: check the code); cache poisoning (DR-029 stub, key spaces); data exposure; token handling (memory-only in the UI, headers, SSE auth limits); resource exhaustion and DoS (SSE stream caps, handshake slots, peer tenant flood DR-027, epoch-map cloning); origin overload and SSRF protections (ORIGIN_URL validation, pinned addresses); malicious requests; dependency and supply-chain risk (Cargo.lock facts; the advisory scan has not been run: verify); failure recovery; lost or delayed invalidation; races (G4A-01); memory safety (Rust; grep for unsafe); performance bottlenecks.

DELIVER the security and failure-scenario matrix with columns: What could go wrong | How | Protection that exists | Where (file::symbol) | Tests that validate it | What remains unresolved (with the G4 finding ID and its disposition from docs/release/g4/cycle1_findings_disposition_register.md). Do not claim a scenario is addressed because a design document says so.

Then performance evaluation: latency, throughput, P50 / P95 / P99 (how percentiles are computed in each place; the inconsistent definitions G4A-24 and F24), cache hit rate (and the hit_ratio_cumulative caveat), origin request reduction, resource consumption, contention, workload generation, benchmark fairness, baselines, methodology. List every real measurement the repo contains (benches/, bench-results/, lab-results/, docs/testing/) with its profile, host, workload, seed, repetitions and limits, and say what it can and cannot support. Never invent numbers. Also reliability: failure handling and health endpoints (src/health.rs).

## Chapter 7: Phantom Lab, Experimentation and Innovation
Explain: what Phantom Lab is and why it was built; the simulation architecture (src/lab/*: the T1 discrete-event simulator t1_sim.rs, virtual clock clock.rs, seeded RNG rng.rs, workloads.rs, origin_sim.rs, matrix_runner and m6_pipeline, eval.rs analysis with BCa bootstrap, the freeze, preregistration and sealed-seed mechanism); deterministic replay; workload scheduling; scenarios and experiment execution (src/lab_live/*: Scenario B, T2, a real gateway on loopback: loadgen, gateway process control, analysis verdicts); metrics and interpretation; reproducibility; evidence retention (lab-results/, manifests, hashes); the Lab API (src/api/lab_api.rs, SSE lab-1) and the UI; the relationship between simulation (T1, SIMULATED) and real backend behaviour (T2, REAL loopback); provenance labels.

THEN innovation and differentiation. Compare with established systems where meaningful (Redis, Memcached, Varnish, groupcache and singleflight, CDN request collapsing, Caffeine, TinyLFU, SIEVE and S3-FIFO literature, consistent and rendezvous hashing, Cassandra-style anti-entropy). Use the literature in docs/research/ and the prior-art sweep (docs/research/rt0_prior_art_sweep_2026-10-30.md, claims_ledger.md); mark literature claims as verified from a primary source or not, per CLAUDE.md. Answer: what is genuinely different; which problems the combination addresses; the actual technical contribution; what is demonstrated; what needs stronger evidence (the M6 negative result, AIMD behaving as a static cap, loopback-only evidence); where established systems are more mature. Do not claim best-in-class, and do not claim distributed caching was invented here. Build an evidence-based innovation argument and an honest weaknesses list.
